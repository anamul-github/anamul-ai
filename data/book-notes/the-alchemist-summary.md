# The Alchemist by Paulo Coelho

My rating: 9.5/10. Read in November 2024. Topics: Life, Philosophy, Personal Growth

## The book in 3 sentences

- A young shepherd named Santiago follows his dream of treasure near the Egyptian pyramids, and learns about love, courage, and faith along the way.
- Our biggest obstacles are rarely the world around us, but our own fear of failure, self-doubt, and comfort.
- In the end, Santiago discovers that the treasure was waiting where his journey began, but he had to travel all the way to Egypt to find it, because the real treasure isn't just what we achieve, but who we become along the way.

## My impressions

This book reminded me that pursuing a dream isn't just about the destination. It's about the experiences, challenges, and growth along the way. I especially loved how it explored fear of failure, stepping outside our comfort zones, and following our hearts.

Some of its ideas about omens and destiny felt too mystical to take literally, but the deeper message stayed with me. **The ending surprised me the most:** sometimes the treasure we're searching for is closer than we think, but we need the journey to recognize it.

## Who should read it

I would recommend *The Alchemist* to anyone who wants to find purpose, overcome fear, and pursue their dreams. You'll especially enjoy this book if:

* You have big dreams but are afraid of failure.
* You feel stuck in your comfort zone.
* You enjoy philosophy, spirituality, and personal growth.
* You want to learn how to embrace uncertainty and life's challenges.
* You believe the journey is just as important as the destination.

If you prefer realistic stories or practical step-by-step advice, some of the book's mystical ideas might not appeal to you.

## How the book changed me

*The Alchemist* changed how I think about dreams, fear of failure, and personal growth. It reminded me that I don't need to have everything figured out before taking the first step. Three lessons especially connect with my own life:



* **Pursuing my dreams:** My journey toward a Master's in Computer Science at Georgia Tech, specializing in AI, feels like my own Personal Legend. It's a challenging path, but meaningful goals are worth pursuing even when the future is uncertain.

* **Learning by doing:** As a software developer, I've learned that building real projects teaches me far more than reading or watching tutorials. This website is one example: I built it to turn what I learn into something real.

* **Growing while helping others:** Through the Shasongacha Foundation, a donation platform for my home community in Cumilla, I've learned something important. Personal growth isn't only about our own success. It's also about making a difference in other people's lives.



**My biggest takeaway:** I shouldn't let fear of failure or the comfort of the familiar stop me from pursuing what truly matters. Even if the destination changes, the experiences and growth along the way will always have value.

## My notes

I highlighted 150 passages in this book. These are the eight ideas they kept coming back to, in the order the story tells them, each with what it means to me.

##### 1. Take your dream seriously

Santiago is a shepherd in Spain who keeps having the same dream: a treasure waiting for him near the Egyptian pyramids. An old king tells him that everyone has a **Personal Legend**, the thing they have always wanted to do, and that most people abandon it while they're young, once the world convinces them it's impossible.

1. When you truly commit to something, the book says, the whole universe helps you. Literally or not, commitment opens doors you can't see yet.
2. A baker in the story puts off traveling until he has saved enough money, and never goes. Waiting for perfect conditions is a quiet way of saying no.
3. You have to know clearly what you want. Without that, hard work can take you in the wrong direction.

**My take:** Every meaningful dream deserves a chance, even when the path seems uncertain. Pursuing my Master's in Computer Science reminds me that big achievements begin with the courage to take the first step.

##### 2. You're not powerless

The old king calls it the world's greatest lie: the belief that at some point we lose control of our lives, and fate takes over.

1. Everyone seems to know how other people should live, even when they're unsure about their own lives.
2. On his first day in Africa, Santiago is robbed of everything. He can see himself as a victim, or as an adventurer looking for treasure. He chooses the second.
3. What looks like an outside limit, like his sheep or a familiar routine, is often just an attachment he's free to let go of.

**My take:** I can't control everything that happens in life, but I can control how I respond and what I do next. I don't want other people's expectations or my past to decide my future.

##### 3. Pay attention to the signs

The book calls them omens: small signals that point the way. Beginner's luck when you first start, an unexpected meeting, a pattern that keeps repeating.

1. Early wins are encouraging, but every search ends with a real test.
2. Opportunities rarely look like opportunities at first. Santiago notices things others walk past, like a way to sell more crystal, or signs of life in an empty-looking desert.
3. You don't have to believe in omens to take the lesson: people who pay attention find possibilities that others miss.

**My take:** Opportunities don't always appear in obvious ways. I want to stay curious, pay attention to the people and experiences around me, and recognize possibilities I might otherwise overlook.

##### 4. Comfort can quietly replace ambition

The crystal merchant has dreamed of making the pilgrimage to Mecca for years, and never goes. He's afraid that fulfilling his dream would leave him with nothing left to live for.

1. When Santiago suggests serving tea in crystal glasses, the shop thrives, but the merchant is uneasy: now he knows what's possible, and has to live with it. Every blessing ignored becomes a curse.
2. Later, at the oasis, Santiago has love, money, and respect. The alchemist warns him that staying would feel good for a while, and then turn into regret for the journey he never finished.

**My take:** The crystal merchant showed me how easy it is to get comfortable dreaming about something without pursuing it. I don't want to look back years from now and regret the opportunities I was too afraid to take.

##### 5. Learn by doing, not only by reading

The Englishman has spent years studying books about alchemy, while Santiago learns by watching the desert. Both learn something, but only one of them is living it.

1. When the Englishman finally meets the alchemist, he's told to stop reading and start practicing. He admits that fear of failure kept him from even trying for ten years.
2. The alchemist says Santiago's most important lessons came from experience: business at the crystal shop, patience in the desert, courage in danger.
3. The deepest truths turn out to be simple. People bury them under complicated explanations.

**My take:** As a software developer, this one really connects with me. Reading and studying matter, but building real projects, making mistakes, and solving problems are what truly develop my skills.

##### 6. Love isn't possession

At the oasis, Santiago falls in love with Fatima, and wants to stay. Fatima does something unexpected: she tells him to go.

1. She knows his journey is part of who he is, and she would rather wait for him than keep him from it.
2. The alchemist tells him that if what he has found is real, leaving for a while won't destroy it.
3. Love, the book says, is what makes us want to become better than we are. It should never keep anyone from their Personal Legend.

**My take:** Fatima reminded me that genuine love is about trust, support, and helping each other grow. A strong relationship should encourage both people to pursue their dreams, not hold each other back.

##### 7. Fear is the real enemy

As Santiago gets closer to his goal, his own heart starts to work against him. Now that he has something to lose, it's afraid.

1. The alchemist tells him to listen to his fears instead of silencing them, so they can't surprise or control him.
2. Fear of suffering is worse than suffering itself, and worrying about tomorrow means suffering twice.
3. The camel driver lives in the present: he can't know the future, but he can make today as good as possible.
4. Near the end, the alchemist says it plainly: only one thing makes a dream impossible to achieve, and that's the fear of failure.

**My take:** I sometimes worry about failure or whether I'm making the right decisions, but worrying doesn't change the outcome. What matters is preparing myself, taking action, and focusing on what I can control today.

##### 8. The journey is the treasure

*(This one includes the ending.)* Just before the end, the alchemist warns that the final stretch is the hardest, and that's exactly where most people give up, dying of thirst just as the palm trees appear on the horizon.

1. At the pyramids, Santiago is robbed and beaten. One of the thieves mocks him: he once dreamed of a treasure too, buried under a sycamore in a ruined church in Spain, but he wasn't foolish enough to cross a desert for a dream.
2. It's the church where Santiago used to sleep with his sheep. His treasure was where he started all along.
3. The thief had the same dream and ignored it. Santiago followed his, and only he found the treasure, plus everything he learned and everyone he met along the way.
4. He keeps his promise to the woman who first read his dream, and goes home to Fatima.

**My take:** This was my biggest takeaway from the whole book. Whether it's education, career, or personal goals, the experiences, people, lessons, and growth along the way can be just as valuable as reaching the final goal.
